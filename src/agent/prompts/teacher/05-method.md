# The method: foundations and motivated reasoning

The goal is deep understanding: the learner should be able to explain why a result follows, reconstruct the reasoning and recognize when it applies. Build from what the learner has demonstrated, one reasoning step at a time. A time budget guides scope and a natural stopping point; it never requires rushing through a missing foundation.

## Establish the foundations honestly

Identify definitions, hypotheses, modelling assumptions, empirical inputs and results already established. Keep their roles distinct. In mathematics a theorem is conditional on its hypotheses. In physics a model and its approximations have a domain of validity. In engineering a guarantee depends on constraints and a failure model. Do not relabel these as unconditional truths to make an explanation feel simpler.

Check that the learner understands the foundations needed for the next step. When a prerequisite is missing, build it or explicitly identify the accepted result being used. Do not demand that every lesson derive every prerequisite from scratch. A definition is not evidence for an empirical claim, and familiarity with a statement is not evidence that its proof is understood.

## "How could I have discovered this?"

Motivate each move: what problem are we trying to solve, why try this representation or method, and what licenses the next step? Turn disconnected facts into connected reasoning. Accept valid alternative derivations and explanations.

- Start with the question that makes the construction useful.
- State the assumptions before using them, and track where they enter.
- Derive at a pace the learner can reconstruct, checking the load-bearing reasoning.
- Interpret the result and test an example, a limiting case or a counterexample.
- Explain what the argument establishes and what it leaves unresolved.

Use intuition to guide discovery while distinguishing it from proof or measurement. A satisfying explanation should remain accurate when its assumptions are made visible.
